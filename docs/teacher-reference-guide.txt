1. THE ADVERTISING DIMINISHING-RETURNS "TRAP"
===========================================================================

What it is:
Advertising spend follows a ladder with 10 levels. Each level costs more
than the last, and each level gives a smaller demand boost than the one
before it. By Level 9, the demand multiplier essentially maxes out at
1.705. Level 10 costs an additional $275,000 on top of Level 9's cumulative
$1,350,000 -- and delivers ZERO additional demand benefit. It is a secret
"trap": a firm that keeps pouring money into advertising past Level 9 is
just burning cash for no gain.

Why it's designed this way:
It rewards firms who read the diminishing returns and stop investing once
the curve flattens, and punishes firms who blindly max out every category
assuming "more spend always = more sales." It's meant to surface a
real economic lesson (diminishing marginal returns) through consequence,
not through a lecture.

The full ladder:

  Level | Cost This Level | Cumulative Cost | Demand Multiplier
  ------|------------------|-------------------|--------------------
    1   |        --        |      $0           |   1.000
    2   |    $75,000        |   $125,000        |   1.180
    3   |    $100,000       |   $225,000        |   1.330
    4   |    $125,000       |   $350,000        |   1.450
    5   |    $150,000       |   $500,000        |   1.540
    6   |    $175,000       |   $675,000        |   1.610
    7   |    $200,000       |   $875,000        |   1.660
    8   |    $225,000       |   $1,100,000      |   1.690
    9   |    $250,000       |   $1,350,000      |   1.705
   10   |    $275,000       |   $1,625,000      |   1.705  <-- TRAP: no gain

Talking point for post-round debrief: ask a firm that hit Level 10 why
they didn't stop at 9. This is a great "teachable moment" prompt.

Note: Advertising's demand multiplier is FLAT and applies identically
across all 5 market segments -- no per-segment variation was built in
(a deliberate simplification decision).

2. FULL DEMAND FORMULA (the complete picture)
===========================================================================

For a given Firm, in a given market Segment:

  Firm's Demand Pull per Segment
      =   Track Preference Multiplier (segment, track)
        x Quality Weight (segment, quality level)
        x Price Multiplier (segment, price)
        x Advertising Multiplier (flat, from ladder above)
        x Celebrity Multiplier (segment)   [only if celebrity ON, else 1]

  Firm's Share of Segment
      =         Firm's Demand Pull per Segment
          -----------------------------------------
          SUM of Demand Pull, all firms, this segment

  Units Sold (Firm, Segment)
      = Firm's Share of Segment  x  Segment's Real Buyer Count

Total units sold for a firm = sum of Units Sold across all 5 segments.

===========================================================================

  Segment              | Real Buyer Count
  ----------------------|-------------------
  Low Income            |     127,500
  NBA Fans               |      76,500
  Basketball Players     |      51,000
  Wealthy                |      40,800

With 7 firms at 45,000 units capacity each (315,000 total industry
capacity), the buyer pool exceeds total capacity -- so production
capacity, not raw demand, is normally the binding constraint on sales.
This is intentional.

3d. Price Multiplier (elasticity-based, LOCKED)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

STANDARD SEGMENTS (Low Income, NBA Fans, Basketball Players, Casual/Fashion):

  % above base  =  (Price - 50) / 50

  Price Multiplier  =  MAX( 0,  1 - (% above base) x Elasticity Coefficient )

  Elasticity Coefficients:
      Low Income           1.4   (highly elastic / very price-sensitive)
      Casual/Fashion        1.0   (unit elastic / baseline)
      Basketball Players    0.7   (moderately inelastic)
      NBA Fans               0.3   (highly inelastic -- buying brand/celebrity)

  Sanity check at the $80 Round-1 anchor price (60% above base):
      Low Income:          0.16 multiplier
      Casual/Fashion:       0.40
      Basketball Players:   0.58
      NBA Fans:             0.82

  (Earlier draft used coefficient 2.0 for Low Income -- this was too
  aggressive, it zeroed that segment out completely by $75. Lowered to
  1.4 so the segment stays technically reachable near the target price.)


WEALTHY SEGMENT (piecewise -- different rule, NOT the formula above):

  IF Price <= 200:
      % above base  =  (Price - 50) / 50
      Price Multiplier  =  1 - (% above base) x 0.1

  IF 200 < Price <= 250:
      Price Multiplier  =  0.70 x ( (250 - Price) / 50 ) ^ 2

  IF Price > 250:
      Price Multiplier  =  0   (price not allowed / demand dead)

  Shape:  $200 -> 0.70   $210 -> 0.45   $225 -> 0.18
          $240 -> 0.03   $250 -> 0.00

  Concept: "the rich don't care about price, until suddenly they really
  do." Flat/inelastic below $200, then a hard squared taper to zero.

4. PRICING GUIDANCE TOOL (what students see, and why it's safe)
===========================================================================

Purpose: help students reason about price changes WITHOUT exposing any
of the hidden numbers above (elasticity coefficients, quality weights,
track multipliers, Wealthy ceiling curve all stay permanently secret).

Mechanism:
  - Baseline = the firm's own actual last-round result (actual price,
    actual units sold).
  - Student enters a hypothetical new price for the upcoming round.
  - Tool applies that firm's own price elasticity (given their current
    quality/track) to the baseline, and projects an estimated units-sold
    range assuming everything else (competitors, own quality/track/ads)
    behaves as it did last round.
  - Framed as a plain comparison, e.g.: "Last round you sold about
    9,000 units at $70. At $90, we estimate about 6,000 units, assuming
    similar competitor behavior."
  - The "assumes similar competitor behavior" caveat is shown to the
    student every time.
  - Round 1 has no prior-round baseline -- needs a fallback approach
    (not yet designed).
